The River Arts District, Honestly

What's open, what's new, and what's still gone — two years after the water came through.

← Back to all Asheville picks

Updated August 2026

I drop people in the River Arts District most weeks, and the question in the back seat is always some version of the same one: is it even worth going anymore? The answer is yes, and it comes with conditions. This page is the version I'd want if I were the one visiting.

Everything below reflects the district as of late August 2026. RAD changes faster than any other part of Asheville right now — buildings reopen, projects stall, tenants move. I re-check this page regularly, but call ahead for anything specific. If you find something wrong here, tell me and I'll fix it.

The short version

The River Arts District is open. Around twenty studio buildings are operating, hundreds of artists are back at work, and in 2026 USA Today named it one of the top arts districts in the country. It is also, still, a construction site in places. You will walk past gutted brick warehouses with high-water marks on them on your way to a gallery full of people making things.

Both of those are true at the same time. Visitors who show up expecting the polished 2019 version get thrown. Visitors who show up understanding they're walking through a neighborhood mid-rebuild have a much better day — and, from what artists tell me, spend more money, which is the part that actually matters.

What actually happened here

On September 27, 2024, Helene pushed the French Broad River to a crest of 24.67 feet. The River Arts District sits on the floodplain of that river, in a narrow valley, in one of the rainiest regions in North America. The buildings that make the district charming — century-old tanneries, cotton mills, warehouses — are there precisely because industry wanted to be next to the water and the railroad.

That geography decided who lost what. The railroad tracks run through the district and function as a rough dividing line. Upper RAD, on higher ground east of the tracks, largely survived. The riverside blocks did not. Some buildings were gutted to the studs. plēb urban winery's building was physically washed off its foundation.

Artists lost inventory, tools, kilns, presses, and in many cases decades of work. Some never came back. That's not a detail to skip past — the arts council was warning even before the storm that high costs were already pushing artists out, and the flood accelerated it.

If you want the deeper version of why this valley floods the way it does — orographic rainfall, steep terrain, thin soils over old bedrock — that's on the Blue Ridge geology page. The short answer is that this is one of the wettest places in the country and the water has nowhere to go but the French Broad.

Where to go right now

These are the buildings currently open. Most house multiple artists, so each one is worth twenty minutes to an hour depending on how much you like talking to makers.

  • 348 Depot StPink Dog Creative OpenNever flooded, which made it a lifeline for the district. Around 35 artists in studios, plus a gallery that runs real exhibitions. Fresh West wood-fired pizza and Crusco are both in the building. Studios are generally open to the public 11–5 most days. If you only have time for one stop, make it this one.
  • 36 Foundy StMarquee OpenThe big one. A 50,000-square-foot former industrial building filled with artists, makers, vintage dealers and antique vendors. Devastated by the flood, reopened in September 2025, and drew over 5,000 people on its first day back. Roughly 350 artists exhibiting. Budget more time than you think.
  • 375 Depot StAura Arts OpenMulti-gallery space housing Trackside Studios, Jaime Byrd Contemporary Art, and the Clayworks ceramics collective. One of the easiest buildings to browse and one of the best for actually watching people work.
  • 122 Riverside DrCotton Mill Studios OpenOne of the district's oldest and largest studio buildings. Took significant damage and needed more than a year of remediation; artists are back. El Patio by Guajiro, a Cuban-inspired beer and wine bar, occupies the first floor.
  • Lyman StWedge Studios OpenArtists upstairs, Wedge Brewing downstairs, and now Summit Coffee and Joyful Noise Hi-Fi in the same storefront cluster. Probably the single best block in the district for combining art, coffee, beer and food without moving your car.
  • Depot StNorthLight Studios OpenA longtime favorite, and one visitors consistently rank above the downtown art museum for the simple reason that the artists are right there and will talk to you.
  • 140 Roberts StNC Glass Center OpenPublic-access glass studio and gallery. You can watch glassblowing, and they run short workshops — including 30-minute make-your-own sessions. Best hands-on option in the district for people who don't think of themselves as artists.
  • RADOdyssey Center for Ceramic Arts OpenSerious clay operation with a gallery, classes, and residencies. Pair it with Clayworks at Aura if pottery is your thing.
  • 17 Foundy StFoundation Woodworks OpenA co-op of area woodworkers. After the flood they restarted under a donated industrial tent set up on the concrete slab where plēb used to stand, which is about as RAD a detail as exists. Shelves, bowls, furniture, even guitars.
  • RADAlso openPhil Mechanic Building (experimental and contemporary work, includes STAND Gallery), Warehouse Studios (seven artists — painting, fiber, jewelry, paper), RAD Rendezvous, Riverside Studios, The Hatchery Studios, The Lift Studios, The Wyre Shops, Local Cloth, Rite of Passage + Sew Co., 362 Depot Street, and 20 Artful Way.

New since the flood

This is the part most guides miss. Several of the best reasons to go to RAD right now did not exist before Helene.

  • Wedge StudiosSummit Coffee + Joyful Noise Hi-Fi NewSummit's old Foundy Street shop was destroyed — the river went through the walls. They ran a coffee truck for eighteen months and reopened indoors in April 2026, now sharing a space with Joyful Noise Hi-Fi, a vinyl listening lounge and kitchen from the founders of Hi-Wire Brewing. Records behind the bar, coffee by day. This is the new heart of that block.
  • Foundy StCultivate Climbing NewA bouldering gym in the old indoor skatepark building. It was weeks from opening when the flood hit and had to start over. It's open now, and the outdoor Foundation skatepark next door survived and is busy daily.
  • 122 Riverside DrEl Patio by Guajiro NewBeer and wine bar on the ground floor of Cotton Mill Studios, front patio and back porch, sister concept to the long-running Guajiro Cuban food truck. Good landing spot after a Cotton Mill browse.
  • Artful WayRAD Yoga NewA bright studio from longtime instructor Kristin Mitchell. Part of a small cluster of wellness openings in the district over the past year.

Two larger projects are in motion and worth knowing about, with the caveat that neither is a sure thing on any particular timeline. Salvage Station, the outdoor music venue, is working toward a return to the district on a site near the French Broad, though the building intended for it was hit by a fire, which complicated the plan. And Burial Beer acquired Curve Studios, while GBX Group took over Riverview Station and a run of Foundy and Lyman Street properties in a preservation-focused redevelopment. What all that means for the district's character is a live argument among people who live here.

What's still closed

Do not build an afternoon around these. As of now, still shut or under reconstruction:

  • Riverview Station — one of the anchor studio buildings, now in redevelopment under new ownership
  • Curve Studios — the former Standard Oil buildings, closed pending redevelopment
  • Foundation Studios
  • Level 42 Gallery and Studio
  • 26 Foundy, 372 Depot Street, and 8 River Arts Place

347 Depot is listed as opening soon. Some businesses relocated out of the district entirely and won't be coming back, and a number of restaurants that people specifically ask me for — the RAD locations of some well-known spots — are still not operating even though their locations elsewhere in town are fine. Check before you make it the plan.

The one link worth bookmarking: the artists' association keeps a live open/closed list by building at riverartsdistrict.com. It is more current than any travel article, including this one. I check it before I recommend a specific building to a passenger.

Driver's notes: how to actually do this

  • Get dropped on Depot Street. It's the reliable spine of the open part of the district. From there Pink Dog, NorthLight, Aura Arts and 362 Depot are all walkable, and you can work toward Foundy or Lyman from there.
  • Parking is worse than it was. Lots that used to be reliable are now staging areas, fenced, or gone. Wedge's locations have their own free parking, which makes them a good anchor if you're driving yourself. Otherwise this is a genuinely good neighborhood to be dropped at.
  • Friday and Saturday are the days. More studios keep public hours then. Midweek you'll find plenty open but also plenty of locked doors with a phone number on them. January through March is the thinnest stretch of the year.
  • Give it three hours, not one. The most common mistake is treating RAD as a quick stop between breweries. Marquee alone can absorb an hour. If you've got ninety minutes, pick two buildings and do them properly.
  • Wear real shoes. Sidewalks are inconsistent, there's active construction, and some of the best stuff involves crossing gravel and rail crossings.
  • Bring a card and be willing to use it. This is the whole point. The district's recovery is being paid for substantially by people buying art from the people who made it. A $40 mug does more here than a $40 tour.
  • Ask about the flood, but read the room. Most artists will tell you their story and want to. Some are tired of it. Let them lead.
  • Don't photograph the damaged buildings as scenery. People lost businesses in those. This is a working neighborhood, not a disaster exhibit.

Eating and drinking without leaving

You can build a whole evening here. Wedge Brewing has two locations in the district and both are back, with food trucks in rotation. The Bull and Beggar sits in the Wedge Studios cluster for a more serious dinner. Crusco does seasonal Italian on Depot Street. Fresh West runs wood-fired pizza out of Pink Dog. El Patio handles beer and wine at Cotton Mill. Summit Coffee and Joyful Noise cover the coffee-into-evening transition.

That's enough to spend a full day in a district that, two years ago, a lot of people assumed was finished.

Why I still send people here

Because it's the most honest thing in Asheville right now.

Downtown looks the way it always looked. Biltmore looks the way it always looked. The River Arts District looks like what actually happened to this city, and it also looks like what people decided to do about it. You can stand on Foundy Street and see a high-water line above your head on one building and a gallery with three hundred artists in it across the street.

Nobody here needs your sympathy. They'd much rather have your attention and, if something on the wall speaks to you, your money.
